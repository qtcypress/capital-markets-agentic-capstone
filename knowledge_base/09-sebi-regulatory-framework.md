# SEBI Regulatory Framework

## Scope and Status of This Document

This document is an educational summary of how the Indian securities regulatory framework applies to equity derivatives, written in the author's own words, and it is not legal advice, investment advice or a statement of current regulation. Answers derived from this document should be presented as educational summaries only. Regulation in this area changes frequently through SEBI circulars on the F&O framework, exchange rulebook amendments and clearing corporation notices, and any figure, threshold or requirement described here may have been superseded.

No circular numbers, dates or verbatim regulatory text are reproduced here. Where a specific quantitative requirement is described, it is illustrative and is marked as such. Verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this.

The framework has three practical layers. The statutory and regulatory layer consists of securities law and the regulations and circulars issued by the Securities and Exchange Board of India. The self-regulatory layer consists of the rules, byelaws and regulations of the recognised stock exchanges and clearing corporations, which operate as front-line regulators of their members. The contractual layer consists of the agreements between brokers and their clients, which typically impose requirements stricter than the regulatory minimum.

A market participant is bound by all three simultaneously. A practice permitted by regulation may still be prohibited by an exchange rule or a broker's client agreement, and compliance with the loosest of the three is not compliance. Anyone with an actual regulatory question should consult the primary sources and qualified professional advice rather than relying on this summary.

## SEBI's Role and Mandate

The Securities and Exchange Board of India is the statutory regulator of the Indian securities market, and its mandate combines three functions that shape everything in the derivatives market: protecting the interests of investors, promoting the development of the securities market, and regulating it. These three sit in tension — investor protection often argues for restriction while market development argues for access — and much of the observable regulatory activity in equity derivatives is SEBI resolving that tension.

SEBI's regulatory tools include registering and supervising intermediaries such as brokers, clearing members, depository participants and research analysts; prescribing conduct and disclosure requirements; approving new products and product features before exchanges may launch them; setting risk management norms including margining frameworks and position limits; conducting inspections and investigations; and taking enforcement action ranging from monetary penalties to debarment.

In the derivatives context, SEBI's approval is required for the introduction of a derivative on a new underlying, for changes in contract design, and for material changes in the risk management framework. Eligibility criteria for a stock or index to have derivatives — turnover, market-wide position availability, delivery-based liquidity and impact-cost measures — are set through SEBI circulars on the F&O framework and applied by the exchanges through periodic review.

SEBI also sets the framework within which exchanges and clearing corporations act as first-line regulators, requiring them to maintain surveillance systems, enforce member obligations, maintain settlement guarantee resources and report to SEBI. This document summarises these arrangements at a conceptual level for educational purposes and is not a substitute for the primary regulatory sources.

## Exchanges and Clearing Corporations as Front-Line Regulators

Recognised stock exchanges and clearing corporations in India function as front-line regulators of the derivatives market, and understanding their role explains why so many practical rules come from the exchange rather than from SEBI directly. The exchange operates the trading platform, admits and supervises trading members, sets contract specifications within SEBI's framework, runs real-time surveillance, and enforces its own rules, byelaws and regulations against members.

The clearing corporation performs the risk function. It novates every trade, becoming central counterparty to both sides; it computes and collects margin under the SPAN-based framework; it runs the daily mark-to-market cycle; it manages final settlement including physical delivery for single-stock derivatives; and it maintains a settlement guarantee fund and a default waterfall specifying the sequence in which resources are consumed if a clearing member defaults.

Because these institutions sit closest to the market, they set most of the parameters a trader encounters day to day: lot sizes, tick sizes, strike intervals, price operating ranges, quantity freeze limits, margin percentages, haircut schedules, delivery margin staging and settlement timetables. All of these are revised as market conditions change, sometimes at short notice.

The structural separation between exchange and clearing corporation, and the requirement that clearing corporations maintain their own capital and governance, is intended to insulate settlement integrity from trading-platform commercial pressures. This is an educational description of institutional roles, not a statement of any institution's current rules; verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this.

## Eligibility of Underlyings for Derivatives

Eligibility criteria determine which shares and indices may have derivatives listed on them, and the framework is designed to ensure that a derivative is written only on an underlying liquid enough to price it and to settle it. The criteria are set through SEBI circulars on the F&O framework and applied by exchanges at periodic review.

The general shape of the criteria for single stocks includes a minimum period of listing and trading in the cash segment; median quarter-sigma order size, which measures the rupee value that can be traded without moving the price by a defined amount; market-wide position limit capacity, computed from free-float market capitalisation; and average daily delivery value in the cash segment. Thresholds are periodically raised to keep the eligible universe aligned with market growth.

Stocks that fail the criteria on review are moved to an exit process: no new contract series are introduced, and existing contracts run off to expiry, after which the stock has no derivatives. Stocks that newly meet the criteria may be introduced. There is typically a cooling period before a stock that has exited can re-enter.

For indices, eligibility considers the index's constituent liquidity, the transparency and rules-based nature of its construction, and the availability of a reliable settlement value. Weekly option availability on an index is a further product decision layered on top of basic eligibility, and the set of indices carrying weekly options has been revised.

All specific thresholds are illustrative and change; verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this. This is an educational summary and not a determination of any security's current eligibility.

## Investor Categorisation and Suitability Requirements

Investor categorisation in the Indian derivatives framework determines what a participant may trade and what documentation the intermediary must obtain, and it rests on the principle that a leveraged product requires demonstrated capacity to bear its risks. Broadly, participants are categorised as retail individual investors, high-net-worth individuals, corporates, and institutional participants including domestic institutions and registered foreign portfolio investors, with proprietary trading by members treated separately.

Before a client may trade derivatives, the broker must complete know-your-customer verification, obtain a risk disclosure acknowledgement covering the specific risks of leveraged derivatives, and collect evidence of financial capacity — commonly income proof, net worth declaration, bank or demat statements — as required by exchange norms. Documentation must be refreshed periodically.

SEBI has additionally required that risk disclosures be presented to clients in a standardised form, including disclosure of the proportion of individual traders who incur losses in the equity derivatives segment, so that the leveraged nature of the product is made explicit rather than inferred. Brokers must also maintain records demonstrating that the client understood the disclosure.

Suitability in the Indian framework is largely procedural rather than advisory: the intermediary must verify capacity and disclose risk, but an execution-only broker does not owe a recommendation duty. Where a registered investment adviser or research analyst is involved, separate regulations govern that relationship, including restrictions on conflicts and on performance claims.

This document does not assess any person's suitability for derivatives trading and offers no investment advice. Documentation requirements and disclosure formats change; verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this.

## Prohibited Practices and Market Conduct

Market conduct rules prohibit a defined set of practices in the Indian securities market, and they apply to derivatives as fully as to cash equities. The core prohibitions concern fraudulent and unfair trade practices, insider trading, and manipulation of prices or volumes.

Insider trading regulation prohibits trading in securities while in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information relating to a company or its securities, and derivatives on that company's shares are within scope. Designated persons of listed companies and their connected persons face additional restrictions including trading windows, pre-clearance requirements and disclosure obligations. The framework also requires companies to maintain structured digital databases of information sharing.

Manipulation prohibitions cover a range of behaviours: creating a false or misleading appearance of trading, matched orders and circular trading between colluding parties, marking the close by trading near the session end to influence a settlement or closing price, spoofing and layering by entering orders without intention to execute, and disseminating false information to influence prices. In derivatives specifically, attempts to influence an underlying's closing price on expiry day, so as to affect settlement of derivative positions, fall squarely within the manipulation prohibitions — one reason settlement values are computed from averaged windows rather than single prints.

Front-running, in which a person trades ahead of a known large order, is prohibited and is a recurring enforcement theme in derivatives markets. Exchange surveillance systems monitor for these patterns and refer cases to SEBI.

This document is an educational summary of prohibited conduct categories; it does not define the legal boundaries of any prohibition and is not legal advice. Anyone with a specific conduct question should consult the primary regulations and qualified counsel.

## Algorithmic and Automated Trading Rules

Algorithmic trading in the Indian derivatives market is subject to a specific approval and control framework, because automated order generation can amplify errors and destabilise markets at machine speed. The framework applies to strategies where orders are generated automatically, whether by an institutional trading desk, a broker's platform, or a retail client using an application programming interface.

Core requirements include exchange approval of algorithms before deployment, with the member submitting a description of the strategy and its risk controls; unique identification of algorithmic orders so that surveillance can attribute them; mandatory order-level risk controls at the broker including price checks, quantity checks, order-value checks and cumulative-exposure checks; and a kill-switch capability allowing the member to disable an algorithm immediately.

Order-to-trade ratio limits discourage excessive order messaging relative to executed trades, with penalties applied where ratios exceed thresholds. Co-location and proximity hosting arrangements are subject to fair-access requirements, including audit trails and standardised access terms.

SEBI has separately developed a framework for retail participation in algorithmic trading through broker-provided application programming interfaces, addressing registration of algorithms, attribution of API-originated orders, responsibility of the broker for controls, and the treatment of third-party algorithm providers. The details of this framework, including registration thresholds and the treatment of self-developed versus vendor algorithms, have evolved through successive consultations and circulars.

Because this area changes rapidly, the shape rather than the specifics is what should be retained: automated derivatives trading in India requires exchange-approved algorithms, broker-level pre-trade risk controls, order attribution and a kill switch. Verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this. This is an educational summary and not compliance advice.

## Client Funds, Securities and Segregation

Segregation requirements govern how brokers hold client money and securities, and they exist because the failure of a broker should not put client assets at risk. The core principle is that client funds and securities are held for the client and may not be used for the broker's own purposes or to meet another client's obligations.

Practically, brokers must maintain client bank accounts separate from proprietary accounts, must not use one client's credit balance to fund another client's debit, and must transfer securities purchased by clients to the clients' own demat accounts within prescribed timelines. Upstreaming requirements direct that client funds be moved to clearing corporations rather than retained at the broker beyond permitted levels.

For collateral, the margin pledge mechanism keeps securities in the client's own demat account with a pledge created in favour of the broker and clearing corporation, replacing earlier arrangements in which securities were transferred into broker-controlled accounts. The client authorises the pledge through a depository-level confirmation, which creates an audit trail independent of the broker.

Client-level position and collateral reporting to the clearing corporation supports segregation by making each client's positions and collateral visible at the clearing layer rather than only in aggregate at the member level. This underpins the ability to protect and, in a default scenario, potentially port non-defaulting clients' positions.

Running account settlement requirements periodically return unused client funds rather than allowing indefinite retention by the broker. Timelines, thresholds and procedural details in all of these areas are prescribed and revised; verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this. This document summarises the principles for educational purposes and is not legal advice.

## Surveillance, Reporting and Disclosure

Surveillance in the Indian derivatives market operates at three levels — exchange, clearing corporation and regulator — and generates a set of reporting and disclosure obligations that shape participant behaviour. Exchanges run real-time and end-of-day surveillance for unusual price and volume movement, concentration of positions, patterns consistent with manipulation, and breaches of position limits.

Participants face disclosure obligations of their own. Clients whose positions exceed defined thresholds in a derivative contract may be required to disclose their positions to the exchange, and failure to disclose is itself a violation. Substantial acquisition and takeover regulations, and insider trading disclosure requirements for designated persons, can be triggered by derivative positions as well as by cash holdings, since economic exposure rather than legal ownership is often the relevant test.

Members report client-level positions and collateral daily, enabling the clearing corporation to monitor limits at client level rather than only in aggregate. Margin reporting includes the peak intraday requirement, and shortfalls are reported and penalised.

Publicly, exchanges disseminate open interest by contract, market-wide position limit utilisation for single stocks, category-wise participant activity, and alerts where a security approaches a ban threshold. These disclosures are data rather than signals, and interpreting them as predictive of price direction is inference by the reader, not a claim made by the exchange.

## Grievance Redressal and Dispute Resolution

Grievance redressal in the Indian securities market follows a structured escalation path, and knowing it matters because derivatives disputes — disputed trades, unauthorised orders, erroneous square-offs, margin disputes — are comparatively common in a leveraged, fast-moving segment. The first step is the intermediary's own complaint process, which registered brokers must operate with defined response timelines.

If unresolved, the investor may escalate to the exchange's investor grievance mechanism, which examines the complaint and can facilitate resolution between the member and the client. SEBI operates a centralised complaint registration and tracking system through which complaints are routed to the relevant intermediary or exchange with monitored timelines, and SEBI has also established an online dispute resolution framework for market disputes, providing conciliation and arbitration through recognised online platforms.

Beyond conciliation, exchanges operate arbitration mechanisms with panels and defined procedures, and awards are enforceable subject to the applicable appellate process. Exchanges also maintain investor protection funds that can compensate eligible claims arising from a defaulting member, subject to defined limits and eligibility conditions.

Two practical points affect outcomes. Documentary evidence matters greatly — order logs, contract notes, ledger statements, communications — so participants should retain records. And timelines apply at each stage; a complaint raised long after the event may be time-barred.

Procedures, forums, timelines and compensation limits are prescribed and revised; verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this. This is an educational description of the redressal architecture and is not legal advice about any specific dispute.

## How to Use Regulatory Information Responsibly

Regulatory information about Indian equity derivatives should be used with an explicit awareness of its shelf life, because the framework is revised continuously and the practical parameters most often quoted are the ones that change most often. Margin percentages, position limits, lot sizes, eligibility thresholds, penalty rates, delivery margin schedules and expiry conventions have all been revised repeatedly.

A responsible approach has four elements. First, distinguish structure from parameters: the existence of SPAN-based margining, of physical settlement in single-stock F&O, of market-wide position limits and of the ban-period mechanism are structural and stable, while the numbers attached to them are not. Second, always cite the general source — SEBI circulars on the F&O framework, exchange rulebooks, the exchange contract specification master — rather than inventing a circular number or date, which manufactures false precision. Third, mark every quantitative claim as illustrative and direct the reader to verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on it. Fourth, never present a summary as a compliance determination.

For an automated application built on this knowledge base, the practical rule is that regulatory content should support explanation, not decision. Explaining what a market-wide position limit is, and that a ban period restricts new positions, is appropriate. Asserting that a particular stock is currently in a ban period, or that a particular position complies with a limit, requires live data from the exchange and is outside what a static document can support.
