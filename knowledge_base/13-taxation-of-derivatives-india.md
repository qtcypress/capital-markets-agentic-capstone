# Taxation of Derivatives in India

## Scope and Status of This Document

This document is an educational summary of how Indian tax rules generally apply to exchange-traded equity derivatives, and it is not tax advice, legal advice or investment advice. Tax treatment depends on a person's specific facts — residential status, nature and scale of activity, other income, choice of tax regime, applicable assessment year — and on law that changes with each Finance Act. Answers derived from this document should be presented as educational summaries only, and any actual tax position should be determined with a qualified chartered accountant or tax professional.

Every rate, threshold and limit stated here is illustrative and may have been superseded. Securities transaction tax rates, presumptive taxation turnover thresholds, audit thresholds, loss carry-forward periods and advance tax instalment percentages have all been revised in recent years. Verify against the current exchange/SEBI circular, the current Finance Act and the Income Tax Department's current guidance before relying on this.

No section numbers of the Income Tax Act are cited in a way that asserts their current content, and no circular numbers are invented. Where a statutory concept is named — business income, speculative transaction, presumptive taxation, tax audit — it is described conceptually rather than by reproducing statutory text.

The structural points that are stable and worth retaining are these: exchange-traded equity derivatives income in India is generally treated as business income rather than capital gains; it is generally non-speculative business income despite the colloquial use of the word speculation; turnover for derivatives is computed on a special basis rather than as contract value; and securities transaction tax is a separate transaction-level levy independent of income tax.

## Business Income Versus Capital Gains Treatment

The classification of derivatives income as business income rather than capital gains is the foundational point of Indian derivatives taxation, and it flows from the nature of the instrument. A derivative contract is not held as a capital asset in the ordinary sense; it is a contract entered into and settled, typically within a short period, and the prevailing treatment is that gains and losses from exchange-traded equity derivatives constitute income from business or profession.

The practical consequences of business income treatment are substantial. Business income is taxed at the taxpayer's applicable slab rates for individuals and Hindu undivided families, or at the applicable corporate rate for companies, rather than at the concessional short-term or long-term capital gains rates that apply to listed equity shares. There is no holding-period distinction, so no long-term treatment exists for derivatives regardless of how long a position is held.

Business treatment also permits deduction of expenses incurred wholly and exclusively for the purpose of the business. Commonly claimed items include brokerage, exchange transaction charges, SEBI turnover fees, stamp duty, goods and services tax on those charges, data and terminal subscriptions, internet and telephone costs attributable to the activity, professional fees, depreciation on equipment used, and interest on borrowed capital used in the business. Securities transaction tax is deductible as a business expense where the income is assessed as business income.

Because classification depends on facts and on the taxpayer's overall pattern of activity, and because a person may have both an investment portfolio taxed as capital gains and a derivatives activity taxed as business income, the boundary requires professional judgement. This is an educational summary and not a determination of any person's classification.

## Speculative and Non-Speculative Business Income

The distinction between speculative and non-speculative business income matters because losses from a speculative business can generally be set off only against speculative income, while non-speculative business losses enjoy broader set-off. The terminology is counterintuitive in the derivatives context.

Indian tax law defines a speculative transaction as one settled otherwise than by actual delivery, which would appear to capture derivatives. However, an exception carves out transactions in derivatives carried out on a recognised stock exchange, and the effect is that exchange-traded equity derivatives income is generally treated as non-speculative business income notwithstanding that the contracts are cash-settled or netted.

By contrast, intraday equity trading in the cash segment — buying and selling the same share on the same day without taking delivery — is generally treated as speculative business income, because it does not fall within the derivatives exception.

The distinction produces a practical asymmetry that traders frequently encounter. A loss in NIFTY options, being non-speculative, can generally be set off against other business income and, subject to conditions, against income under other heads in the same year, with the balance carried forward. A loss in intraday cash equity trading, being speculative, can generally be set off only against speculative gains, and its carry-forward period is shorter.

A person who trades both derivatives and intraday cash equity therefore maintains two separate computations within the same business. The precise conditions, exceptions and interactions are matters of law that change; verify against current provisions and consult a qualified professional. This is an educational summary and not tax advice.

## Turnover Computation for Derivatives

Turnover computation for derivatives is a specialised and frequently misunderstood area, and it matters because turnover determines whether presumptive taxation is available and whether a tax audit is required. Turnover for derivatives is not contract value or notional value — a common and expensive misconception.

The generally followed approach, reflected in guidance issued by professional bodies, computes turnover for futures and options as the absolute sum of favourable and unfavourable differences on settled transactions. In other words, every trade's profit or loss is taken at its absolute value and all such absolute values are added together, so a profit of 20,000 and a loss of 15,000 contribute 35,000 to turnover rather than 5,000.

For options, guidance has at various times additionally included premium received on sale of options in the turnover computation, and has at other times excluded it. Treatment of this element has been revised in successive editions of professional guidance, and it materially changes computed turnover for option writers. Verify the current position with a qualified professional before relying on any single approach.

An illustration of the absolute-difference method: a trader executes twenty round trips over a year with individual results of plus 30,000, minus 22,000, plus 15,000, minus 40,000 and so on. Net profit might be a modest figure while turnover, being the sum of absolute values, could be several lakh rupees. This is why active traders with small net results can nevertheless cross audit thresholds.

Notional value is never turnover. One lot of NIFTY at 24,000 with lot size 75 has a notional value of 18,00,000 rupees, but a trade producing a 5,000-rupee profit contributes 5,000 to turnover, not 18,00,000. All methodology described is illustrative; verify against current professional guidance.

## Presumptive Taxation and the Small Trader

Presumptive taxation is a simplified scheme under which eligible small businesses declare income at a prescribed percentage of turnover rather than maintaining full books and computing actual profit, and derivatives traders whose turnover is within the eligible limit may fall within its scope. The scheme is optional, and choosing it has consequences beyond the current year.

The general shape is that a resident individual, Hindu undivided family or partnership firm carrying on an eligible business with turnover up to a prescribed limit may declare income at a prescribed percentage of turnover — an illustrative six percent where receipts are through banking channels and eight percent otherwise. The prescribed turnover limit has been raised over time, with a higher limit available where cash receipts and payments are below a specified proportion. All figures illustrative; verify against the current Finance Act and consult a qualified professional.

Two conditions attach. Declaring under the scheme and then opting out in a later year can lock the taxpayer out of the scheme for a defined number of subsequent years. And declaring income below the presumptive percentage, where total income exceeds the basic exemption limit, generally triggers a requirement to maintain books and obtain a tax audit — the very obligations the scheme was meant to avoid.

For a derivatives trader with a loss, the interaction is significant: a loss cannot be declared under a presumptive scheme that deems a minimum profit, so a trader wishing to claim and carry forward a loss will generally need to maintain books and may need an audit.

This is an educational description of a scheme's structure and not a recommendation to adopt or avoid it. Eligibility, rates, limits and lock-in periods change; professional advice is essential.

## Tax Audit Requirements

Tax audit is the requirement that a taxpayer's accounts be examined and certified by a chartered accountant, and derivatives traders encounter it because the turnover computation described earlier produces large turnover figures relative to net results. Whether an audit is required depends on turnover, on whether presumptive taxation has been used, and on whether declared income falls below the presumptive percentage.

The general structure has several triggers. Turnover above a prescribed threshold requires audit, with a higher threshold available where cash receipts and cash payments are each below a small proportion of total receipts and payments — a condition most derivatives traders satisfy easily, since all transactions flow through banking channels. Separately, a taxpayer who has previously opted into presumptive taxation and subsequently declares income below the presumptive percentage, with total income above the basic exemption limit, generally faces an audit requirement regardless of turnover.

Illustrative thresholds have been in the region of one crore rupees for the base limit and ten crore rupees where the digital-transaction condition is met, but these have been revised and the conditions attached to them are detailed. Verify against the current Finance Act and consult a qualified professional.

Practical implications for traders are three. Audit requires proper books of account — trade-wise records, ledgers, bank statements, contract notes, expense vouchers — maintained through the year rather than reconstructed afterwards. Audit has a cost and a statutory due date, and late filing attracts penalty. And because audit applicability turns on the turnover figure, the method used to compute turnover directly determines whether audit applies, which is another reason the option-premium question in turnover computation matters.

This is an educational summary of audit triggers and not a determination of any person's requirement.

## Set-Off and Carry-Forward of Losses

Set-off and carry-forward rules determine what a derivatives trader can do with a loss, and because derivatives losses are generally non-speculative business losses, the rules are comparatively favourable — but they are conditional.

Within the same year, a non-speculative business loss can generally be set off against income from any other business, including speculative business income, and against income under other heads subject to restrictions, notably that business losses cannot be set off against salary income. The order and permissibility of inter-head set-off is governed by statute and has exceptions.

Losses not absorbed in the current year may be carried forward and set off against business income in subsequent years, subject to a prescribed carry-forward period — illustratively eight assessment years — and subject to a critical procedural condition: the return of income for the year in which the loss arose must be filed within the due date. A late return generally forfeits the right to carry forward the business loss, which is one of the most consequential and most commonly missed points in derivatives taxation.

Speculative losses, such as those from intraday cash equity trading, carry forward for a shorter period and can be set off only against speculative income.

Two further points. Carried-forward business losses can generally be set off only against business income in later years, not against other heads. And where a taxpayer has opted into a tax regime with restricted deductions, the treatment of certain losses and allowances differs, so the regime choice interacts with loss planning.

Periods, conditions and regime interactions change with each Finance Act; verify against current provisions and consult a qualified professional. This is an educational summary and not tax advice.

## Securities Transaction Tax on Derivatives

Securities transaction tax, commonly abbreviated STT, is a transaction-level levy collected by the exchange on specified securities transactions, and it is entirely separate from income tax. It is charged whether the transaction is profitable or not, and it is collected at the time of trade rather than assessed annually.

For equity derivatives the levy is structured by product and by side. On futures, STT is charged on the sell side, computed on the traded value, at an illustrative rate expressed as a small percentage of turnover. On options, STT is charged on the sell side computed on the option premium, at a higher illustrative percentage. Critically, on options that are exercised, STT is charged on the buy side computed on the settlement value — that is, on the intrinsic value or, under some formulations, on the full contract value — at a rate that produces a materially larger charge than premium-based STT.

Rates have been revised more than once in recent Finance Acts, including increases to the option-sale and futures-sale rates. All rates here are illustrative and no specific percentage should be relied upon; verify against the current Finance Act and the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this.

The exercise-side charge is the point that causes real losses. A deeply in-the-money option carried to exercise can attract STT computed on settlement value that consumes a large fraction of the option's intrinsic value, and in extreme cases can exceed the gain. This is a principal practical reason traders close in-the-money option positions in the market rather than allowing exercise.

Where derivatives income is assessed as business income, STT paid is generally allowable as a business expense. This is an educational summary, not tax advice.

## Other Statutory Charges and Their Tax Treatment

Beyond securities transaction tax, several statutory charges attach to derivatives transactions in India, and understanding them completes the picture of transaction cost and of what may be claimed as a business expense.

Exchange transaction charges are levied by the exchange on turnover, with different illustrative rates for the futures and options segments, and options charges computed on premium turnover rather than notional. SEBI turnover fees are a small levy on turnover. Stamp duty is charged on the buy side at rates that were harmonised across states, with different illustrative rates for futures and options. Goods and services tax is charged on brokerage and on exchange and SEBI charges, at the applicable rate.

Brokerage itself is a commercial charge rather than statutory, and structures vary from a flat fee per order to a percentage of turnover, with the flat-fee model widespread in Indian retail derivatives.

For a trader assessed under business income, all of these are generally deductible as expenses incurred for the business, along with securities transaction tax, provided they are properly evidenced through contract notes and broker statements. Goods and services tax paid may or may not be creditable depending on the trader's own registration status and the nature of the activity, which is a question for professional advice.

The aggregate of these charges is material for high-frequency or multi-leg strategies. A four-legged iron condor opened and closed involves eight order-level charge events, and on a structure whose maximum profit is a few thousand rupees per lot the total can be a meaningful fraction. All rates are illustrative and change; verify against the current Finance Act and the current exchange/SEBI circular before relying on this.

## Advance Tax and Compliance Calendar

Advance tax is the requirement to pay income tax during the year in which income is earned rather than only at filing, and derivatives traders are commonly liable because their income is not subject to tax deduction at source in the way salary is. The general rule is that a taxpayer whose estimated tax liability for the year exceeds a prescribed threshold — an illustrative ten thousand rupees — must pay advance tax in instalments.

The conventional instalment structure requires cumulative payment of specified percentages of the estimated annual liability by four dates through the financial year, with illustrative cumulative proportions of fifteen percent, forty-five percent, seventy-five percent and one hundred percent. Shortfall against these instalments attracts interest, computed monthly on the deficiency, and a separate interest charge applies to shortfall in total advance tax paid by year end.

The estimation problem is genuine for derivatives traders, because trading results are volatile and the year's outcome is unknown at each instalment date. Practical approaches involve re-estimating at each instalment date on the basis of results to date plus a conservative projection, and paying the higher of the computed instalment and a prudent buffer, since interest on excess payment is not charged in the same way as on shortfall.

The wider compliance calendar for a derivatives trader includes maintaining books through the year, obtaining a tax audit where applicable by its due date, and filing the return by the applicable due date — which, as noted earlier, is a precondition for carrying forward business losses.

All thresholds, percentages, dates and interest rates are illustrative and change with each Finance Act; verify against current provisions and the Income Tax Department's current guidance.
